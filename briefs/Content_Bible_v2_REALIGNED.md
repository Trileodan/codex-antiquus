ALEX-01  •  359 BC
Philip Builds the Machine
Alexander's story begins before Alexander. In 359 BC, Philip II becomes king of Macedon after a military disaster leaves the kingdom vulnerable. Greece to the south is divided; Macedon is not yet the master of it.
Philip rebuilds the army around the sarissa, an exceptionally long pike, but the pike is only part of the system. The phalanx fixes an enemy in place. Cavalry, lighter troops and flexible infantry exploit the problem it creates. Macedon's strength is combined arms, not one magic weapon.
By the time Alexander inherits the kingdom, he will inherit an army designed to create openings — and commanders trained to use them.
Remember the pattern: hold them with the phalanx; break them somewhere else.

ALEX-02  •  338 BC
Chaeronea: Greece Is Beaten
Philip's rise eventually forces a showdown with a coalition led by Athens and Thebes. At Chaeronea, Macedon wins decisively. The teenage Alexander, about eighteen, plays an important role in the battle, although the surviving accounts do not let us reconstruct every movement with confidence.
The result matters more than the heroic details. Philip can now organise most of the Greek states through the League of Corinth and prepare the project he has wanted for years: war against the Persian Empire.
Sparta remains conspicuously outside the settlement. Macedon has not erased Greek identity. It has become the dominant power above a collection of fiercely independent Greek states.
338 BC: Chaeronea. Philip controls the Greek political landscape.

ALEX-03  •  336 BC
A Murder Creates a King
Two years later Philip is assassinated at Aegae by Pausanias, one of his bodyguards. Ancient writers offer motives and conspiracies, but the full story remains uncertain.
Alexander is about twenty. His first problem is not Persia. It is survival. Rivals exist at home and Greek states may interpret Philip's death as their chance to escape Macedonian control.
Alexander moves quickly, secures the throne and reasserts Macedonian dominance. The invasion Philip planned will now belong to his son.
Philip's death also leaves the planned Persian invasion in an awkward state: Macedonian forces have already been sent into Asia, but the political system behind them has suddenly lost its king. Alexander's speed in securing recognition at home is therefore not just personal survival. Every month spent fighting for the throne gives Greece, neighbouring peoples and Persia more time to exploit Macedonian weakness.

ALEX-04  •  335 BC
Thebes Learns How Fast Alexander Moves
A rumour that Alexander has died encourages revolt at Thebes. Alexander responds with one of the qualities that will define his career: speed. He appears before the city far sooner than his enemies expect.
Thebes falls and is treated brutally. Ancient sources report thousands killed and tens of thousands enslaved, although exact figures should be treated cautiously. Most of the city is destroyed.
The message is deliberate. Alexander can be merciful when mercy is useful, but rebellion behind him cannot be allowed while he is preparing to cross into Asia.
Before Alexander can conquer Persia, he makes sure Greece will not explode behind him.
The destruction of Thebes is also political theatre aimed at everyone else. Athens has opposed Macedon too, but Alexander does not destroy every hostile city. By making one spectacular example and then accepting the submission of others, he combines terror with restraint. It is a pattern worth remembering: his violence is often extreme, but it is frequently connected to a strategic message.

ALEX-05  •  334 BC
Across the Hellespont
Alexander crosses into Asia with a Macedonian-led army and immediately enters Achaemenid Persian territory. The Persian king Darius III is not waiting on the beach. Local Persian commanders must respond first.
At the Granicus River, Alexander forces a crossing and wins. Ancient accounts dramatise the personal danger, including the tradition that Cleitus the Black saves Alexander during the fighting.
The strategic result is clear: the invasion has survived its first test. Alexander can now move down the western coast of Asia Minor, weakening Persian control and securing his rear.
334 BC: Granicus. The invasion is no longer a plan; it is a war.
The invasion is usually presented as Alexander's great adventure, but it is also Philip's unfinished project. Macedon can claim revenge for the Persian invasions of Greece more than a century earlier, while conquest offers wealth, prestige and a way to keep the Greek states focused on an external enemy. Alexander's personal ambition matters, but he is stepping into a war whose political foundations already exist.

ALEX-06  •  333 BC
Issus: Alexander Meets Darius
At Issus, Alexander finally faces Darius III in person. The geography matters. The battlefield is constrained, reducing the advantage of Persia's larger forces.
Alexander attacks toward the Persian centre and the position of Darius. When Darius leaves the battlefield, Persian resistance collapses. His family falls into Alexander's hands.
Alexander treats the royal family with conspicuous respect. That is not merely kindness. He is beginning to behave not just like a raider defeating Persia, but like a rival king capable of inheriting it.
333 BC: Issus. Darius escapes; his family does not.
Issus also changes the diplomatic balance. Darius can still raise new armies and offers terms at different stages of the war, but Alexander's ambitions are growing with his victories. A settlement that might once have looked astonishingly generous becomes less attractive when Alexander begins to believe the whole empire is obtainable.

ALEX-07  •  332 BC
Tyre: The Island That Would Not Open Its Gates
Tyre is a formidable island city off the Phoenician coast. Alexander wants access and control; Tyre refuses the terms under which he wishes to enter.
The problem is brutally simple: Alexander has an army, and the city is in the sea.
His engineers build a causeway from the mainland toward the island while Tyrian defenders attack the works from ships and walls. As Alexander gains naval support from other Phoenician cities, the siege tightens. After roughly seven months, Tyre falls.
The aftermath is severe. Ancient casualty and enslavement figures vary and should not be treated as precise, but Alexander makes another unmistakable example of prolonged resistance.
332 BC: when the city is an island, Alexander changes the coastline.

ALEX-08  •  332–331 BC
Egypt Welcomes a New Pharaoh
After Tyre and Gaza, Alexander enters Egypt, which has been under Persian rule. He encounters little organised resistance and is accepted as ruler.
He founds Alexandria on the Mediterranean coast and travels to the oracle of Amun at Siwa. Later writers turn the visit into a story about divine parentage and Alexander's identity. What the oracle actually told him is unknowable.
Egypt gives Alexander wealth, legitimacy and a new way of presenting himself. To Macedonians he is king. In Egypt he can also be pharaoh. His empire is already forcing him to become different things to different peoples.
This ability to adopt local forms of legitimacy will become one of the central tensions of Alexander's reign. To Egyptian priests, presenting the new ruler through pharaonic tradition is normal statecraft. To some Macedonians, later Persian-style court practices will feel much more threatening. The empire is forcing Alexander to answer a question he never faced as king of Macedon alone: how does one man rule peoples with completely different political traditions?

ALEX-09  •  331 BC
Gaugamela: The Persian Empire Breaks
Darius chooses a broad plain near Gaugamela and prepares it for cavalry and scythed chariots. Persia can use its width; Alexander cannot simply stretch his smaller army to match it.
Alexander moves obliquely, drawing Persian forces outward. When a gap appears, he drives the Companion cavalry toward the Persian centre. Darius eventually flees.
Elsewhere the battle remains dangerous, and Alexander's army does not win by pressing a single magic button. But Gaugamela destroys Darius's ability to defend the heart of the empire.
Babylon, Susa and Persepolis follow. Alexander has won Persia's great capitals. The question now changes: is he still conquering Persia, or becoming its king?
331 BC: Gaugamela. The war of invasion becomes a struggle over who inherits Persia.

ALEX-10  •  330 BC
The Death of Darius Changes the War
Darius retreats east. One of his own satraps, Bessus, seizes him and eventually abandons him mortally wounded as Alexander closes in.
Alexander gives Darius a royal burial and presents himself as the legitimate successor punishing a regicide. Bessus claims the throne as Artaxerxes V, but Alexander now has a powerful political argument for continuing east.
This is an important shift. Revenge for the Persian invasions of Greece can no longer explain everything. Alexander is defending a claim to the entire Achaemenid inheritance.
For Alexander's Macedonian followers, however, the justification is becoming less obvious. The Persian king has been defeated, the great capitals have fallen and the original invasion has succeeded. Continuing toward Bactria and Sogdiana increasingly serves Alexander's claim to universal Achaemenid succession — and his own refusal to leave a rival claimant unconquered.

ALEX-11  •  330–327 BC
Conquering Central Asia Is Not Gaugamela Again
Bactria and Sogdiana do not offer Alexander one convenient king and one decisive battlefield. Resistance is mobile, local and difficult to pin down. Spitamenes uses raids, horsemen and local support to punish Macedonian detachments.
Alexander adapts with smaller mobile columns, garrisons, rapid marches and local alliances. The campaign is hard and often brutal.
He also marries Roxana, daughter of the Bactrian noble Oxyartes. Conquest is increasingly mixed with incorporation. Alexander is no longer merely passing through the Persian Empire; he is trying to rule its peoples.
This matters because it destroys the comforting idea that Alexander simply wins a sequence of neat set-piece battles. In the east he faces something closer to an insurgency: distance, difficult terrain, scattered strongholds and opponents who can disappear rather than accept one decisive contest. Macedonian superiority remains formidable, but the kind of war has changed.

ALEX-12  •  328 BC
Cleitus and the Cost of Becoming 'Great'
At a drinking party, Alexander quarrels with Cleitus the Black — the veteran associated in tradition with saving his life at Granicus. The argument turns violent and Alexander kills him.
Ancient accounts emphasise Alexander's subsequent remorse. Whatever details were embellished, the episode captures a growing tension around the king: Macedonian companions who remember Philip's court are watching Alexander adopt elements of Persian royal behaviour and demand greater deference.
Winning an empire has changed the relationship between Alexander and the men who helped him win it.
Cleitus therefore matters beyond the tragedy of one drunken argument. He represents the old Macedonian companion culture in which the king is first among warrior aristocrats, not an untouchable eastern monarch. Alexander increasingly wants a court capable of ruling an enormous former Persian empire. The people closest to him do not all agree on what that should mean.

ALEX-13  •  326 BC
Hydaspes: Elephants on the Other Bank
In the Punjab, Alexander faces King Porus across the Hydaspes River. Porus watches the obvious crossings and has war elephants — a weapon the Macedonians cannot simply treat as unusually large cavalry.
Alexander repeatedly creates uncertainty about where he will cross, then moves a force upstream and crosses under difficult conditions. Porus turns to meet him.
During the battle, Macedonian light troops and infantry work against the elephants and their crews while cavalry pressure develops around the field. Wounded and frightened elephants eventually contribute to disorder on both sides.
Porus is defeated but retained as a subordinate ruler and given additional territory. Alexander has found another use for a defeated king: make him part of the system.
326 BC: Hydaspes. Defeat Porus — then use Porus.

ALEX-14  •  326 BC
The Army Says No
At the Hyphasis River, Alexander wants to continue east. His army does not.
They have campaigned for years, crossed enormous distances, fought through monsoon conditions and heard reports of powerful kingdoms and many more elephants farther east. The men have reached their limit.
Alexander argues. He withdraws to his tent. He waits. The army does not change its mind.
Eventually the man who has made kings and cities yield to him yields to his own soldiers. The expedition turns back.
Alexander can defeat Darius. He cannot order exhausted men to want another continent.
The refusal is not a mutiny aimed at replacing Alexander. The soldiers have followed him farther than any Macedonian army before them and will continue following him home. What they reject is the endless expansion of the campaign. That distinction matters: Alexander remains king and commander, but for once his authority cannot manufacture willingness where exhaustion has removed it.

ALEX-15  •  325 BC
The Return Is Not a Victory Lap
The journey back includes one of Alexander's most reckless episodes. During fighting against the Mallians, he gets ahead of his troops, enters a defended position with only a few companions and is struck by an arrow in the chest. He nearly dies.
Later, part of the army crosses the Gedrosian desert while Nearchus leads a fleet along the coast. The march is disastrous, with severe losses from heat, thirst and exhaustion. Ancient explanations for Alexander's choice vary; stories that he wished to outdo legendary predecessors may preserve something of his competitive self-image, but practical strategic motives also mattered.
Alexander survives. Many of his people do not.
The Gedrosian episode is especially useful as a warning against treating Alexander's career as an uninterrupted sequence of genius decisions. His operational daring can produce extraordinary results, but the same appetite for difficulty, speed and personal glory can also expose his army to avoidable danger. 'Great' does not mean infallible.

ALEX-16  •  324 BC
Susa and Opis: Whose Empire Is This?
Alexander stages mass marriages at Susa between Macedonian officers and Persian noblewomen and continues integrating Iranian elites into his forces. Thousands of young Iranians have been trained in Macedonian military methods.
To Alexander, this can look like building an empire that will survive conquest. To Macedonian veterans, it can look like replacement.
At Opis the tension explodes. Veterans resent plans to send some of them home and the growing prominence of Persians. Alexander responds furiously before reconciliation is eventually achieved.
The problem is now obvious: Alexander has conquered a multicultural empire faster than anyone has agreed what belonging to that empire means.
The marriages at Susa will not all survive Alexander's death, which tells us something about the limits of imposed integration. Alexander can order ceremonies and promotions; he cannot instantly create a shared Macedonian-Persian identity. Opis is therefore not just a veterans' pay dispute. It is a confrontation over who owns the empire that conquest has created.

ALEX-17  •  323 BC
Babylon: The Empire Loses Its Centre
Alexander reaches Babylon while planning further campaigns. He falls ill and dies at only thirty-two, shortly before his thirty-third birthday.
The cause remains uncertain. Disease is generally more plausible than the elaborate poisoning stories preserved by later writers, but a confident modern diagnosis is impossible.
He leaves no secure adult successor. Roxana is pregnant. His half-brother Arrhidaeus has a dynastic claim but is described by ancient sources as having significant cognitive limitations. The generals possess the armies.
Alexander conquered an empire. He did not solve succession.
323 BC: Alexander dies. The Hellenistic world begins with an argument over what he has left behind.
Later stories claim that, when asked who should inherit the empire, Alexander answered 'the strongest'. The tradition is famous but uncertain and may reflect the wars that followed rather than a reliable deathbed statement. What is certain is that no succession mechanism exists strong enough to make the generals accept one uncontested ruler.

ALEX-18  •  323–305 BC
The Successors
A compromise recognises Philip III Arrhidaeus and, after his birth, Alexander IV. Real power is fought over by Alexander's generals — the Diadochi, or Successors.
The empire fragments through repeated wars. Ptolemy takes Egypt and even intercepts Alexander's funeral procession, bringing the body into his own territory. Other commanders build their own power bases across the former empire.
By 305 BC, Ptolemy openly takes the royal title as Ptolemy I Soter. The unified empire is gone. What remains is a Hellenistic world of competing Macedonian-led kingdoms.
Alexander → Successors → Ptolemaic Egypt, Seleucid Asia, Antigonid Macedon.
This is why Alexander belongs across several Sets rather than inside one. His conquests destroy the Achaemenid Empire, expand Macedonian power, create new Greek-speaking elites in Egypt and Asia, and produce kingdoms that will later collide with Rome. His death is not the end of the story. It is the point at which several new stories begin.

CAR-01  •  4TH–3RD CENTURIES BC
Before Rome Was a Mediterranean Power
Carthage is already an established western Mediterranean power while Rome is still fighting for control of Italy. Its wealth comes from trade, agriculture, taxation and a network of overseas possessions and allies.
Carthage's political system is not a simple monarchy. Powerful aristocratic families, elected magistrates and councils all matter. Its armies often combine Carthaginian leadership with troops recruited from many different peoples.
In Sicily, Carthage has spent generations competing with Greek cities. Rome is not yet the obvious enemy. That changes when a local crisis in Sicily pulls both powers into the same war.
Its North African heartland is also agriculturally rich. Carthage should not be imagined as a city that survives only by merchants sailing around the Mediterranean. Landed wealth, estates and agricultural production sit alongside maritime commerce. That combination helps explain why the city can recover economically even after losing much of its overseas empire to Rome.

CAR-02  •  264 BC
The First Punic War Begins
The immediate crisis centres on Messana in Sicily and the Mamertines who control it. Appeals for outside help draw both Carthage and Rome into the dispute.
What begins as a local intervention becomes a war between the dominant naval power of the western Mediterranean and an Italian land power that has barely begun operating overseas.
Rome's problem is obvious: Sicily cannot be won by pretending the sea is not there.
Neither side begins the crisis with a master plan for a twenty-three-year struggle. Escalation matters. Once Roman troops cross to Sicily and Carthage commits to resisting them, local alliances and strategic fears turn the dispute into something much larger. This is a useful recurring theme in Roman history: enormous wars can grow from decisions that originally look limited.

CAR-03  •  260S BC
Rome Learns to Fight at Sea
Ancient tradition says the Romans used a captured or grounded Carthaginian quinquereme as a model while rapidly building a fleet. The story may simplify a more complicated process, but Rome unquestionably develops major naval capacity with remarkable speed.
The famous corvus boarding device allows Roman infantry to turn some naval encounters into close combat. At Mylae in 260 BC, Rome wins its first major naval victory.
Carthage still has deeper maritime experience. Rome has demonstrated something that will become one of its defining strengths: when an enemy presents a problem, Rome can copy, adapt and keep trying after expensive failures.
The adaptation is expensive. Roman fleets are repeatedly lost not only in combat but in storms, and the human cost is enormous. Yet Rome keeps rebuilding. Carthage's naval tradition gives it advantages in experience; Rome's political system proves unusually capable of absorbing losses and returning with another fleet. The First Punic War becomes an endurance contest as much as a contest of seamanship.

CAR-04  •  241–237 BC
Defeat, Debt and the Mercenary War
The First Punic War ends in 241 BC after Roman victory near the Aegates Islands. Carthage gives up Sicily and accepts a large indemnity.
Then unpaid or underpaid troops revolt in the brutal Mercenary War, sometimes called the Truceless War. Carthage survives, but Rome exploits its weakness to take Sardinia and imposes further financial pressure.
For the Barcid family, this matters. Carthage has lost territory, money and prestige. Hamilcar Barca now looks toward Iberia.
The revolt is not a minor post-war disturbance. Carthage has to fight for survival against men who know its military system and are joined by discontented African communities. Hamilcar's role in defeating the revolt strengthens his prestige. When he later goes to Iberia, he is not simply an adventurer looking for land; he is helping rebuild Carthaginian power after a sequence of humiliations.

CAR-05  •  237–221 BC
The Barcids Build in Iberia
Hamilcar Barca leads Carthaginian expansion in Iberia, creating a new base of manpower, resources and political power. After his death, Hasdrubal the Fair continues the project and founds New Carthage.
Ancient tradition says the young Hannibal swore never to be a friend of Rome. It is a famous story, not something we can verify as a literal childhood oath.
When Hasdrubal is assassinated in 221 BC, Hannibal — still only in his mid-twenties — is chosen as commander.
Iberia gives the Barcids access to silver, soldiers and a theatre in which Carthage can recover without immediately challenging Rome at sea. It also gives the family an unusually strong personal power base. That matters when Hannibal becomes commander: the next war with Rome will be driven not from Sicily but from a Carthaginian system rebuilt in Spain.

CAR-06  •  219–218 BC
Saguntum and the Road to War
Hannibal attacks Saguntum, a city with ties to Rome. After a long siege the city falls. Rome demands satisfaction from Carthage; Carthage refuses.
A useful correction matters here: Saguntum lay south of the Ebro. The outbreak of war cannot simply be reduced to 'Hannibal crossed the treaty line and attacked it'. The diplomacy and treaty obligations are more complicated.
In 218 BC the Second Punic War begins. Hannibal chooses not to wait for Rome to attack Carthage. He takes the war toward Italy.
Rome's relationship with Saguntum and the meaning of earlier agreements remain debated in the sources and modern scholarship. What matters for the learner is that both sides can present the other as responsible for escalation. By the time Roman envoys demand Hannibal's surrender and Carthage refuses, diplomacy has become a test of prestige neither side is willing to lose.

CAR-07  •  218 BC
Hannibal Crosses the Alps
Hannibal moves from Iberia through Gaul and across the Alps into Italy. The crossing is costly through fighting, desertion, terrain and weather, and the exact route remains debated.
The strategic achievement is more important than the tourist question of which pass he used. Rome expected to fight for Spain and perhaps Africa. Hannibal has brought a major army into northern Italy.
Gaulish recruits help rebuild his strength. Rome now has a brilliant enemy operating on Italian soil.
The elephants are the memorable image, but the strategic surprise is more important. Hannibal has bypassed Rome's expected approach and entered a region where recently subdued or hostile Gallic peoples can become allies. He is trying to attack Rome's alliance system from inside Italy, not merely collect battlefield victories.

CAR-08  •  218–216 BC
Three Roman Disasters
Trebia in 218. Lake Trasimene in 217. Cannae in 216.
At Trebia, Hannibal manipulates an eager Roman commander, cold troops and a hidden force under Mago. At Trasimene he turns terrain and poor visibility into a gigantic ambush. At Cannae he allows his centre to bend while his stronger infantry and cavalry create the most famous double envelopment in ancient warfare.
Cannae is catastrophic. But Rome does something Hannibal cannot force it to stop doing: Rome refuses to surrender.
218 Trebia → 217 Trasimene → 216 Cannae.
The three victories are not repetitions of one trick. Trebia uses preparation, bait and concealment. Trasimene is an enormous marching ambush. Cannae manipulates formation, pressure and cavalry superiority. The common thread is Hannibal's ability to understand what the Roman commander is likely to do and shape the battlefield around that expectation.

CAR-09  •  216–209 BC
Why Cannae Does Not End the War
After Cannae, some Roman allies defect, most famously Capua. But the alliance system does not collapse completely. Rome raises more armies, avoids offering Hannibal another ideal decisive battle and continues fighting in multiple theatres.
Hannibal can defeat Roman armies. Destroying Rome's political and manpower system is harder. He also lacks an easy way to storm the heavily defended city of Rome itself.
The war becomes a contest of endurance — exactly the sort of war Rome is unusually good at surviving.
This is one of the most important lessons of the entire Punic story: tactical victory and strategic victory are different things. Hannibal has destroyed an army on a scale that should force many states to negotiate. Rome's institutions, allied manpower and refusal to accept defeat deny him the political result his battlefield success seems to deserve.

CAR-10  •  209–206 BC
Spain Starts Slipping Away
A young Publius Cornelius Scipio takes command in Iberia. In 209 BC he captures New Carthage, the Barcid strategic base, using a coordinated assault that includes troops crossing a shallow lagoon when conditions permit.
In 207 BC Hannibal's brother Hasdrubal reaches Italy with reinforcements, but Roman forces intercept him at the Metaurus. Hasdrubal is killed.
The possibility of the two Barcid armies combining in Italy disappears. Hannibal remains dangerous, but the strategic direction of the war is changing.
The Metaurus is especially damaging because Hannibal's Italian strategy needs reinforcement and wider allied collapse. Hasdrubal's death means the second Barcid army will never join him. Rome, meanwhile, is increasingly able to choose where the war is fought. Scipio's rise will eventually move the decisive theatre away from Italy altogether.

CAR-11  •  202 BC
Zama
Scipio carries the war into Africa and allies with the Numidian king Masinissa. Carthage recalls Hannibal from Italy.
At Zama in 202 BC, Hannibal reportedly fields a large elephant force. Scipio arranges his infantry so lanes can open through the Roman formation, reducing the elephants' ability to smash straight through it. Roman and Numidian cavalry leave the field in pursuit and later return against Hannibal's rear.
Scipio wins. The Second Punic War is effectively decided. The formal peace follows in 201 BC.
Remember 202 for another reason: in China, Liu Bang establishes the Han dynasty in the same year.
202 BC = Zama + beginning of Han.

CAR-12  •  201–151 BC
Carthage Without an Empire
Carthage survives but loses its overseas empire, much of its military freedom and its great-power position. It must pay Rome and cannot wage war without Roman permission.
That does not mean the city becomes poor. Its economy recovers impressively, which eventually alarms some Romans.
Hannibal himself enters Carthaginian politics and becomes suffete around 196 BC. He attacks corruption and reforms finances, helping Carthage meet its obligations. Political enemies accuse him of plotting with Antiochus III; under Roman pressure he flees in 195 BC and never returns.
Hannibal's political career is a useful reminder that he does not disappear after Zama. The great battlefield commander becomes a domestic reformer, and successful financial reforms can make him threatening in a different way. Rome has defeated Hannibal militarily, but it remains deeply suspicious of his ability to rebuild Carthaginian strength.

CAR-13  •  2ND CENTURY BC
Masinissa Keeps Taking
Masinissa, Rome's Numidian ally from the Second Punic War, repeatedly expands at Carthage's expense. Carthage appeals to Rome, but the disputes rarely produce the protection Carthage wants.
The treaty creates a trap: Carthage is expected not to fight without Roman approval, while a Roman ally steadily pressures its territory.
Eventually Carthage fights Numidia without permission. Rome now has the treaty violation it needs.
Masinissa is not simply a Roman puppet. He is building Numidian power and has his own ambitions. Rome benefits from keeping its old enemy constrained and often has little incentive to restrain him decisively. For Carthage, every dispute therefore carries the same frustration: fight back and risk breaking the treaty, or accept continued encroachment.

CAR-14  •  C. 153–149 BC
Cato Wants Carthage Gone
Roman politician Cato the Elder becomes famous for insisting that Carthage must be destroyed. The exact formula usually quoted — 'Carthago delenda est' — is a later conventional wording, but his advocacy is real.
Not every Roman agrees. Scipio Nasica is associated with the opposing idea that a dangerous rival helps keep Rome disciplined.
In 149 BC Rome chooses war. The Third Punic War begins.
Cato's campaign also reveals that Roman fear is not based on Carthage preparing another Hannibalic invasion. The disturbing sight is a supposedly defeated enemy becoming prosperous again. Rome is now powerful enough that the existence of a recovering rival can itself be treated as a strategic problem.

CAR-15  •  149 BC
Rome's Demands Become Impossible
Carthage initially tries to avoid destruction. It provides hostages. It surrenders weapons.
Then Rome demands that the Carthaginians abandon their city and resettle inland.
At that point surrender no longer means accepting Roman dominance. It means accepting the destruction of Carthage as Carthage.
The city chooses resistance and turns itself into a weapons factory. The final war will last roughly three years.
This sequence is essential because it changes the moral and strategic character of the war. Carthage has already complied with demands that make resistance harder. Only after weapons are surrendered does Rome reveal a condition that effectively destroys the community's relationship with its historic city and coastline. Carthaginian resistance becomes desperate because there is very little left to preserve through obedience.

CAR-16  •  146 BC
The End of Carthage
Scipio Aemilianus eventually breaks into the city. Fighting continues through streets, buildings and rooftops for days before the final defenders retreat to the Byrsa.
Large numbers of survivors are enslaved. Ancient figures should be treated cautiously, but the destruction and enslavement are not myths.
The famous story that Romans salted the earth is not ancient history; it is a much later legend.
Polybius, who knew Scipio, preserves the image of the Roman commander reflecting on the destruction and the possibility that Rome might one day suffer the same fate.
In the same year, Roman forces also destroy Corinth in Greece.
146 BC: Carthage and Corinth fall. Rome has almost no peer left in the Mediterranean.

ROM-01  •  4TH CENTURY BC
Rome Before the Mediterranean
While Alexander is conquering Persia, Rome is still fighting for control of Italy. During the Second Samnite War, Rome suffers the famous humiliation at the Caudine Forks in 321 BC, where a trapped Roman army is forced to surrender.
This is useful perspective. The Rome that will eventually dominate the Mediterranean is not yet inevitable.
By the early third century BC, however, Rome has become the dominant power in much of Italy. That success brings it into contact with the other great power of the western Mediterranean: Carthage.
The Samnite Wars teach Rome how difficult the Italian landscape and Italian coalition politics can be. Roman expansion is not a smooth march outward from the city. It is built through defeats, renewed armies, colonies, alliances and repeated wars. That same capacity to lose badly without accepting that the war is over will later become one of Rome's greatest strategic advantages.

ROM-02  •  280–275 BC
Pyrrhus Teaches Rome to Survive Defeat
The Greek city of Tarentum calls on Pyrrhus of Epirus for help against Rome. Pyrrhus is one of the finest commanders of the Hellenistic world and brings war elephants to Italy.
He defeats the Romans at Heraclea and again at Asculum, but his victories are costly. This is the origin of the expression 'Pyrrhic victory': winning the battle at a price that damages your ability to win the wider war.
By 275 BC Pyrrhus leaves Italy. Rome has demonstrated a quality that will later torment Hannibal: defeating a Roman army does not necessarily make Rome stop.
Pyrrhus also exposes Rome to the military world created after Alexander: professional Hellenistic armies, sophisticated combined arms and elephants. Rome can be beaten by that system, but Pyrrhus discovers the strategic imbalance. His own high-quality manpower is difficult to replace; Rome can keep drawing on Italy. The phrase 'Pyrrhic victory' therefore describes more than casualties — it describes a victory that worsens the winner's strategic position.

ROM-03  •  264–241 BC
The First Punic War
Rome's intervention in Sicily pulls it into a long war with Carthage. Rome must learn large-scale naval warfare, absorb enormous losses and keep rebuilding.
Victory at the Aegates Islands in 241 BC ends the war. Sicily becomes Rome's first province outside the Italian mainland.
Rome has crossed an important threshold. It is no longer merely an Italian power.
The war lasts twenty-three years, far longer than either side can have expected at the beginning. Rome suffers repeated naval disasters and still returns to sea. Carthage remains dangerous but ultimately cannot prevent a final Roman fleet from cutting its position in Sicily. Victory gives Rome Sicily and the experience of governing territory beyond Italy — a quiet administrative revolution as well as a military one.

ROM-04  •  218 BC
Hannibal Comes to Italy
The Second Punic War begins after the Saguntum crisis. Hannibal crosses the Alps and wins at the Trebia, Lake Trasimene and Cannae.
After Trasimene, Rome appoints Fabius Maximus dictator. His approach is deliberately frustrating: shadow Hannibal, harass supplies, use terrain, and refuse the decisive battle Hannibal wants.
Romans mock the caution until Cannae demonstrates what another gigantic direct confrontation can cost.
Fabius's strategy is politically difficult because it looks passive. Roman expectations reward aggressive commanders and visible victories; deliberately refusing battle can look like cowardice while farms and allied territory are being damaged. That tension between sound strategy and political pressure helps explain why Rome eventually accepts the gigantic confrontation at Cannae.

ROM-05  •  216 BC
Cannae Does Not Break Rome
Cannae is one of Rome's worst military disasters. Yet Rome refuses negotiation and continues mobilising.
This is not blind heroism. Rome's alliance network, manpower, fortified capital and political determination make total strategic victory far harder than winning a battlefield masterpiece.
The Republic begins learning how to fight Hannibal without giving Hannibal the kind of battle that made him famous.
Some allies do defect after the disaster, so Hannibal's strategy is not foolish. But the core of Rome's Italian network survives. Rome also refuses to ransom many prisoners and takes extraordinary measures to rebuild its armies. The response tells us something fundamental about Roman power: its greatest weapon is not a particular legionary formation but the political system's ability to keep generating war after catastrophe.

ROM-06  •  209–202 BC
Scipio Changes the Direction of the War
Publius Cornelius Scipio attacks Carthaginian power in Iberia, captures New Carthage in 209 BC and later carries the war into Africa.
At Zama in 202 BC, with Numidian support from Masinissa, Scipio defeats Hannibal. He will become known as Scipio Africanus.
Rome has survived the greatest external crisis of the Republic so far. That victory frees Roman attention for the Hellenistic east.
Scipio's importance is strategic as much as tactical. Instead of spending the entire war reacting to Hannibal in Italy, Rome attacks the foundations of Carthaginian power elsewhere. Iberia is stripped away; alliances in North Africa change; Hannibal is eventually forced to leave the battlefield on which he has spent more than a decade tormenting Rome.

ROM-07  •  215–196 BC
Rome Meets Macedon
Philip V of Macedon allies with Hannibal after Cannae, helping trigger the First Macedonian War. Rome cannot afford a giant eastern commitment while Hannibal is in Italy, so Greek allies carry much of the burden.
After Zama, Rome can return with far greater focus. The Second Macedonian War begins in 200 BC.
At Cynoscephalae in 197 BC, broken terrain exposes the limitations of a phalanx that cannot maintain its ideal formation. Roman flexibility proves decisive.
One year later, Flamininus proclaims the 'freedom of the Greeks' at the Isthmian Games. Rome presents itself as liberator rather than permanent occupier — for now.
197 Cynoscephalae → 196 Greek freedom proclamation.

ROM-08  •  192–188 BC
Antiochus Tests Rome
Antiochus III of the Seleucid Empire enters Greece after being invited by the Aetolian League. Rome now faces another Hellenistic great king.
At Thermopylae in 191 BC, Antiochus uses the famous narrow pass and guards the mountain routes, but Roman forces still manage to turn his position.
The war moves into Asia. At Magnesia in 190 BC, the Seleucid army's complex formation unravels as chariots and elephants contribute to disorder and Roman-allied forces exploit the chaos.
The Peace of Apamea in 188 BC strips Antiochus of major territory in Asia Minor and imposes heavy terms. Rome has beaten another successor kingdom.

ROM-09  •  168 BC
Pydna and the Day of Eleusis
In 168 BC Rome demonstrates two different kinds of power.
At Pydna, Lucius Aemilius Paullus defeats King Perseus of Macedon. The Macedonian phalanx is terrifying while intact, but uneven ground opens gaps. Roman units penetrate the broken formation, where the long sarissas become far less useful.
In Egypt, Antiochus IV is threatening Alexandria when Roman envoy Gaius Popillius Laenas orders him to withdraw. Ancient tradition says Popillius draws a circle around the king and demands an answer before he steps out of it. Antiochus backs down.
One year. One military victory. One diplomatic humiliation. Rome can now decide the fate of Hellenistic kings.
168 BC = Pydna + Day of Eleusis.

ROM-10  •  146 BC
146 BC: What Happens When the Rivals Are Gone?
Rome destroys Carthage after the Third Punic War. In the same year, Lucius Mummius destroys Corinth after conflict with the Achaean League.
Rome has become the dominant Mediterranean power.
That changes the central question of our Roman story. The Republic's greatest danger is no longer obviously another Mediterranean state.
It is increasingly the way conquest, wealth, land, military service and political competition are changing Rome itself.
The story changes from 'Can Rome survive its enemies?' to 'Can Rome survive Rome?'
Victory also pours wealth, enslaved people, provincial commands and opportunities for prestige into Roman society. Those benefits are distributed unevenly. The Republic was designed for a city-state and an Italian power; it is now governing a Mediterranean system while its aristocrats compete for rewards on a completely different scale. The next crisis will increasingly come from inside that mismatch.

ROM-11  •  133 BC
Tiberius Gracchus
Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus becomes tribune and attacks a long-running land problem involving public land — ager publicus — concentrated in large holdings.
Another tribune, Marcus Octavius, vetoes his proposal. Tiberius responds by having the assembly remove Octavius from office. The reform passes, but the constitutional precedent is explosive: can the people remove a tribune for using the very veto he was elected to possess?
Tiberius later seeks another tribunate amid accusations that he is aiming at tyranny. A group led by Scipio Nasica attacks him. Tiberius and many supporters are killed without trial.
Rome has crossed a line. Political disagreement has ended in organised political murder.
133 BC: the Republic discovers that killing a political opponent can 'solve' a constitutional crisis.

ROM-12  •  123–121 BC
Gaius Gracchus
Ten years later Tiberius's younger brother Gaius becomes tribune and builds a much broader reform programme: land, grain, roads, colonies, military measures and changes to the courts.
He also confronts a question Rome keeps postponing: what political rights should Rome's Italian allies receive? They fight in Roman wars but many do not possess Roman citizenship.
Opposition mobilises. In 121 BC, after escalating confrontation and violence, the Senate issues its emergency decree — the senatus consultum ultimum. Gaius dies while fleeing and thousands of supporters are reportedly punished.
The Republic has now added state-backed emergency violence to the precedent of political murder.
Gaius is therefore more difficult to reduce to 'Tiberius again'. He is building coalitions across different groups and trying to alter how the Republic distributes food, justice, land and citizenship. His opponents respond politically as well as violently, including rival proposals designed to undercut his popularity. The conflict shows a Republic becoming extremely sophisticated at fighting reform — and increasingly willing to use force when politics fails.

ROM-13  •  113–105 BC
The Northern Threat and Jugurtha
Two crises expose different Roman weaknesses. In North Africa, King Jugurtha of Numidia repeatedly frustrates Roman commanders while accusations of bribery and aristocratic incompetence poison politics at home.
In the north, migrating Cimbri and Teutones defeat Roman forces. At Arausio in 105 BC, rivalry between Roman commanders contributes to a catastrophic defeat.
Rome needs a commander who can win. That brings Gaius Marius to the centre of the story.
Jugurtha's war becomes politically toxic because Romans increasingly suspect that money and aristocratic connections are protecting him from decisive action. The northern disasters create the opposite problem: obvious military incompetence. Together they make the traditional elite look both corrupt and ineffective. Marius can present himself as the practical alternative — a man whose achievements matter more than ancestry.

ROM-14  •  107–100 BC
Marius: The Outsider Becomes Rome's General
Gaius Marius is a novus homo — a 'new man' without a line of consular ancestors. He wins the consulship in 107 BC and takes over the Jugurthine War.
His subordinate Sulla eventually negotiates Jugurtha's betrayal by King Bocchus. Marius receives the commander's glory; Sulla never forgets his own role.
After Arausio, Marius is repeatedly elected consul during the northern emergency. He defeats the Teutones at Aquae Sextiae in 102 BC and the Cimbri at Vercellae in 101.
The traditional idea that Marius single-handedly invented a completely new professional army is too neat. But the period does matter: poorer volunteers, long campaigns and expectations of land and reward strengthen the political relationship between soldiers and successful generals.

ROM-15  •  100 BC
Saturninus: Marius Wins and Still Loses
Marius relies politically on the radical tribune Lucius Appuleius Saturninus, who helps secure veteran settlements. Saturninus and Gaius Servilius Glaucia use increasingly violent political methods.
During the elections, the rival candidate Gaius Memmius is murdered by supporters of the faction. Direct responsibility is difficult to establish, but the crisis is undeniable.
The Senate issues the emergency decree. Marius, as consul, suppresses his own allies. Saturninus and others surrender but are killed by a hostile crowd.
Marius has managed to alienate his former allies without winning the trust of the senatorial establishment.
The episode is another step in our escalation ladder. The Gracchi showed that political opponents could be killed; Saturninus shows organised intimidation becoming part of electoral politics. Marius's response also exposes the trap of personal alliances. Once his allies cross into open violence, his duty as consul collides directly with the network that helped sustain his power.

ROM-16  •  91–88 BC
The Social War
The citizenship question returns under Marcus Livius Drusus the Younger. His reform programme includes extending rights to Italian allies. He is assassinated in 91 BC.
Many allied communities conclude that political reform has failed and revolt. They create institutions of their own, including a capital at Corfinium — renamed Italia — magistrates, coinage and armies.
Rome faces opponents who already understand Roman warfare because they have spent generations fighting alongside Roman armies.
Then Rome does something strategically clever: it begins granting the citizenship many rebels wanted. The political concession helps fracture the revolt even while the military struggle continues.
By the end, citizenship has been extended very broadly across Italy. Rome has fought a terrible war to concede much of the principle it had resisted.

ROM-17  •  88 BC
Mithridates and the Command That Breaks the Republic
Mithridates VI of Pontus overruns Roman positions in Asia Minor while Rome is recovering from the Social War. A large-scale massacre of Romans and Italians follows; ancient casualty figures are enormous and unreliable, but the event is real and politically explosive.
Rome assigns the eastern command to Lucius Cornelius Sulla. Political manoeuvring then transfers it to the ageing Marius.
Sulla is with his army. His soldiers expect the eastern campaign and its rewards.
Sulla makes the decision that changes the rules of Roman politics: he marches his Roman legions on Rome.
88 BC: the first march on Rome. A political dispute is settled by a field army.

ROM-18  •  87–86 BC
Marius and Cinna Learn the Lesson
Sulla seizes Rome, reverses his enemies' measures and then leaves for the war against Mithridates.
His opponents immediately demonstrate the danger of the precedent. Lucius Cornelius Cinna raises forces and joins the returning Marius. They retake Rome in 87 BC, and political enemies are killed in a bloody purge.
Marius becomes consul for the seventh time in 86 BC, then dies only days into the term.
Sulla has proved that armies can decide Roman politics. His enemies have proved the lesson can be copied.
This is why Sulla's first march matters even though he does not remain in Rome. A precedent does not need to create a permanent dictatorship to be destructive. Once one faction proves that an army can overturn politics, the opposing faction has every incentive to find soldiers of its own. The constitutional taboo has become a practical technique.

ROM-19  •  83–82 BC
Sulla Comes Home
After defeating Mithridatic armies in Greece and reaching a settlement with Mithridates, Sulla returns to Italy. He brings hardened veterans and gathers supporters, including the young Pompey and Crassus.
Civil war follows. At the Colline Gate in 82 BC, Sulla's forces win the decisive struggle outside Rome.
This time he is not leaving again. His enemies are defeated and he possesses the power to reshape the Republic.
Sulla's supporters are not one neat ideological bloc. Some are old aristocratic allies; others are ambitious young men who see opportunity in backing the returning general. Pompey raises troops despite lacking the normal authority expected of a commander. Crassus builds his military reputation. The civil war is already teaching the next generation that exceptional service to a powerful general can accelerate a political career.

ROM-20  •  82–81 BC
The Proscriptions
Sulla turns political killing into an organised system. Lists of proscribed enemies are published. A proscribed man can be killed, rewards are offered, property is confiscated and families can suffer political consequences.
The system inevitably creates opportunities for greed and private revenge as well as political punishment.
Thousands die across the Sullan settlement. Exact totals and many individual horror stories come through partisan ancient traditions, but the proscriptions themselves are a fundamental fact.
Rome has moved from mobs killing politicians to a victorious ruler administratively defining which citizens may be killed.
This is more systematic than the earlier street killings that scarred Roman politics. Sulla gives political elimination a legal-administrative form. The state identifies enemies, attaches financial rewards to their deaths and redistributes their property. Violence is no longer merely what happens when a crowd loses control; it becomes a tool through which a victorious regime remakes the political class.

ROM-21  •  82–79 BC
Sulla's Republic
Sulla becomes dictator for the purpose of making laws and reorganising the state. He strengthens the Senate, enlarges it, restricts the tribunate, regularises the career ladder and attempts to impose greater order on magistracies and provincial commands.
His diagnosis is conservative: the traditional senatorial Republic has been undermined by ambitious tribunes and popular politicians, so senatorial control must be restored.
The contradiction is enormous. Sulla uses an army, civil war, proscriptions and extraordinary personal authority to build a constitution intended to stop individuals from overriding the Republic.
Some of Sulla's reforms are serious attempts to make government more predictable: clearer career stages, larger courts, rules around commands and a strengthened Senate. The problem is that the settlement concentrates authority in the very elite whose failures helped produce the crisis, while leaving the military precedent untouched. A future general does not need Sulla's constitution to approve his actions. He only needs soldiers willing to follow him.

ROM-22  •  79–78 BC
Sulla Walks Away
Then Sulla does something remarkable: he resigns the dictatorship.
He does not make himself king. He does not found a hereditary Sullan monarchy. He retires and dies in 78 BC.
That makes his legacy more, not less, important. Sulla apparently believes the Republic can function after him. But everyone has now seen what one general with loyal soldiers can do.
Within a decade major pieces of his constitutional settlement are already being dismantled.
Sulla leaves office. The precedent does not.
His retirement makes Sulla harder to understand as a simple would-be monarch. He appears to believe extraordinary personal power was a temporary instrument used to restore a functioning aristocratic Republic. Yet his actions have demonstrated that the constitution survives only while powerful commanders agree to obey it. That lesson will outlive almost every law he passes.

ROM-23  •  70S BC
The Next Generation
Pompey and Crassus rise rapidly in the world Sulla has left behind. Both benefit from military success, wealth, networks and extraordinary political exceptions.
A third figure is younger but already connected to the old conflict. Julius Caesar is related by marriage to Marius and married to Cinna's daughter Cornelia. Sulla orders him to divorce her; Caesar refuses and is forced into danger before influential supporters secure his pardon.
Later tradition has Sulla warning that there are 'many Mariuses' in the young Caesar. The exact words are literary, but the connection is real: Caesar grows up inside the Republic that Marius and Sulla have broken open.
The important connection is not that Caesar simply copies Sulla. Caesar has watched both sides of the lesson: extraordinary military power can defeat constitutional opposition, but Sulla's terror also creates enemies and memories that survive the dictator. Later, Caesar will make conspicuous use of clemency toward Roman opponents — whether from principle, calculation or both.

ROM-24  •  C. 75 BC
Caesar and the Pirates
While travelling in the eastern Mediterranean, the young Julius Caesar is captured by pirates. Later biographies turn the episode into a perfect Caesar story: he supposedly jokes that their ransom demand is too low, behaves like their superior while captive, and promises to crucify them.
After his ransom is paid, Caesar raises forces, captures the pirates and has them executed.
The polished dialogue comes from later writers, but the underlying episode is generally accepted. It is an early glimpse of the qualities Caesar's career will repeatedly display: confidence, speed, personal audacity and a refusal to accept humiliation.
The episode should not be used as proof that twenty-five-year-old Caesar already has a detailed plan to rule Rome. It is more useful as character evidence. He possesses enormous self-belief and is comfortable improvising authority where formal authority is weak. Those traits will matter much more once military command and Roman politics begin to overlap.

ROM-25  •  73 BC
Spartacus
At Capua, a group of gladiators escapes from a training school. They seize weapons, flee toward Mount Vesuvius and attract growing numbers of escaped slaves and other followers.
Rome initially treats the rising as a limited security problem.
It will become the largest slave revolt in Roman history.
One of its leaders is a Thracian gladiator whose name will survive for more than two thousand years: Spartacus.
Next: the Third Servile War.
The escapees initially use improvised weapons before seizing better equipment from forces sent against them. Their numbers grow rapidly, but the movement is not easy to describe as one unified political revolution. Ancient sources disagree about intentions and internal divisions. What Rome faces is nevertheless serious: a mobile armed force inside Italy repeatedly defeating Roman commanders and attracting people who have every reason not to return quietly to slavery.
